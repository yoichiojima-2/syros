# Brief

A brief is not a summary. A summary tells the reader what a body of material
said; a brief tells them what to do about it. If the reader could finish yours
and still not know what changed or whether it affects them, it is a summary.

## Method

1. **Find the delta first.** Before writing anything, answer: what is true now
   that was not true before? If nothing is, say that in one line and stop —
   a short honest brief beats a padded one, and padding trains readers to skip.
2. **Write the lead last but put it first.** One paragraph: the change, its
   consequence, and the decision it forces. No throat-clearing, no "in this
   brief we will".
3. **Attach evidence to claims, not to paragraphs.** Each sourced claim carries
   its own link or citation inline. A block of references at the bottom lets
   unsupported claims hide between the supported ones.
4. **Separate what you know from what you infer.** Mark inference as inference.
   "Likely", "appears to", and "unverified" are load-bearing words; dropping
   them to sound confident is the most common way a brief misleads.
5. **Cut every sentence that survives deletion.** Read it once with each
   sentence removed. If the brief still works, it stays removed.

See `reference/structure.md` for the section layout and a worked example of the
lead paragraph.

## Length

One page. If the material genuinely does not fit, the brief covers the decision
and links to the detail — it does not grow. Two pages of brief is a report that
has not admitted what it is.

## What to avoid

- Restating the question back to the reader before answering it.
- Hedging every claim uniformly, which carries no information about which ones
  are actually uncertain.
- Ending with "in conclusion". The lead already concluded.
